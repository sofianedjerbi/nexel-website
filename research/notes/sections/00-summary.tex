\section*{Summary}
An AI player for Nexel Worlds, a voxel survival game, should build what a language model describes. In three live
trials it built nothing designed: between the model's goals a learned body, rewarded for first-time events, crafted
away the materials and dug up what had been built (554 digs on tiles it had itself built on, of 750 placements); no
component owned a build to its end; and every builder test started with the materials in hand. A code executor now
owns a drawn build: it gathers the design's bill of materials, then lays it. In the simulator it stands 56 of 60
designs on fresh seeds from an empty inventory (0 before); live, it built a room the game's judge scores 6/6 for working and 8/8 for sense,
then a two-storey house with a gabled roof of shingles and plank eaves and ridge (201 of 203 cells, the last two a
flaw of the design since fixed), then two houses whose best room scores above the best real player's on all three
of the judge's counts. On eight non-building goals over 20 seeds, the goal's code skill succeeds as often as the
learned body or more on all eight, in 2 to 22 times fewer decisions. Two results are proved here and checked in the
game's simulator: the bill the executor gathers is the least raw material a design takes (Theorem 1; the earlier
bill overcounted by up to the recipes' yield), and a flight of stairs reaches its storey exactly when the column
beside its top is laid below it (Theorem 2; this ordering broke the three-storey tower).
